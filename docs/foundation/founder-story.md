

I left my franchise on good terms, meaning I didn't owe them money, in 2011. I had it for two years. I couldn’t book enough business to continue. I have nothing but good things to say about the Right at Home Company that I franchised with. The problem was mine. Ultimately, I was not committed to the business model, and it became difficult to market a product that was sorely needed but was quite expensive. I would not have been able to afford my own service, and neither were most people I talked to. I found that many people did not know that Medicare doesn't cover in-home care. Medicaid does pay for some in-home care, but what they cover is limited.

We did have customers, just not enough of them. I had fantastic staff. We received excellent reviews from our clients.

In my second year, I tried to get resourceful and find services I could offer that were something that the average person needed and could afford. For example, we offered a package that allowed us to be their designated driver after day surgery with a CNA accompanying them home. That was well received but not enough to sustain the operation.

It was in this need for other options that I found the inspiration for the Caring Sensor Community (CSC). I entered this business with an idea that I wanted to do something like the CSC but wasn't sure exactly what that would be. In my graduate work, I found a project from MIT about "Perceptive Presence" (https://web.mit.edu/kkoile/www/papers/Bentley-IEEE03.pdf). One of the items they mention is a Presence Lamp, which is really a pair of lamps. It works like this. This is my lamp that I have at my house in Seattle, and the other lamp is for you in Dallas, mom. When I move around my lamp, yours lights up and when you walk near your lamp, mine lights. It's a simple thing really and can provide significant comfort to a family member. It's like living across the street from your parents, when you see their light come on at the usual time when they get up for their morning cup of coffee. Likewise, not seeing it happen for a couple of days could nudge you to call them.

It clicked for me right away. I knew about wireless sensor networks. I knew about low bandwidth, low voltage devices that were relatively affordable that could be linked together to serve as a network. I didn't think about it as a surveillance system or a medical device, although these same devices are used for that. Many are in market now. It was the idea of the simple nudge of the presence lamp being a relationship device. The same could be true for these other devices, e.g., motion sensors, door switches, household temperature, or weight. There are many others.

In 2011, a lot of technology was still nascent. There were some things that just didn't exist yet. The thing that would really make it all work was social production of content and devices. What makes the Care Sensor Community really work is the intimate understanding that family members, neighbors, and care professionals have for the person. The devices can collect data and report patterns. The community comes to know those patterns and shares them in an open-source library. Now, families who are caring for someone with Parkinson's Disease know patterns that could indicate a change or maybe a problem. They can share these in community under CC-BY-SA Creative Commons license. We can also create our own Open-Source hardware, wireless devices.

I ran out of time and money to pursue this in 2011. I realized though that there is a huge number of people who could benefit from this system. I've been thinking about it ever since.

Then came AI.
